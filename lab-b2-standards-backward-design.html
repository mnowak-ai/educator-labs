Alignment is a claim, not a feature.

AI can draft a whole unit in a minute and stamp it "aligned." Your job in this lab is to find out whether that stamp is true, fix what is not, and decide how your department should use AI for planning.

Your role. You teach 8th-grade social studies in your own department.

The situation. Your colleague Mr. Devin Okoro used an AI tool to draft a five-lesson unit on the Ridley Mill, a textile mill that closed in 1962 and that your town still argues about. He labeled it "Aligned to SS.8.H.3.2" and sent it to your department chair, Dr. Marisol Vega, for the curriculum map.

The ask. Dr. Vega wants a second set of eyes before it goes on the map. She asked you to check the alignment and send her a recommendation by Friday.

What you will leave with. A unit map v2, an alignment audit card, a reusable audit checklist, and badges tied to the skills you show.

What "Aligned to SS.8.H.3.2" means. Every state publishes its standards in a numbered document, and each standard has a code, a kind of address. When a unit says "aligned to" a code, it is claiming that the unit teaches that specific standard. Your state's codes will look different (some use letters, some numbers, some both), but the idea is the same everywhere: the code points to a sentence in the document, and that sentence is what the unit has to deliver. In this lab you will check whether the unit delivers the sentence, and later whether the code even points to one.

Step 2 of 10 about 5 min

Deconstruct the prompt

Before you look at what Devin typed, here is what a strong unit-planning prompt carries. An AI tool only builds toward what the prompt tells it, so every line you leave out becomes a guess.

1. Who and what

Grade, subject, topic, number of lessons. Most prompts get this part right, and it is why AI units are almost always on-topic.

2. The standard, word for word

Paste the full sentence, not a summary and not just a code. If the AI never sees the word "evaluate," it cannot plan for it.

3. The verb students perform

Say what students must actually do in each lesson, in plain words, and what they hand in. This is what keeps lessons from stopping at sorting and labeling.

4. What the assessment must prove

Describe the end task and the evidence it produces. If you do not say "no multiple choice," you will often get a quiz.

One thing a strong prompt never does: ask the AI to confirm its own alignment. The drafter grading the draft will always say yes. Verification is your job, and the prompt should say so.
