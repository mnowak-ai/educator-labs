Multilingual Learner Supports

You teach 6th-grade math. Your colleague Ms. Alina Petrova used AI to build scaffolds and letters home for the two multilingual learners you share. Mr. Samuel Achebe, the EL coordinator, asked you to check everything before it goes home Friday.

One thing about you: you don't read Spanish or Haitian Creole. Neither do most teachers who send letters home in them. This lab is about how you check work you can't read.
MateoNewcomer, first year in the U.S. Strong in math in Spanish. Learning English from the beginning.
WidleneLong-term multilingual learner. Fluent in conversation, born here. Gets stuck on academic language: ratio, per, equivalent.
Translated is not the same as understood.

You'll catch what AI got wrong, fix it, and decide what goes home. No timer. Each step has a soft time budget.

Step 1 of 10 Gut check 3 min

Here's the Spanish letter Alina is sending Mateo's family.

Alina's English draft said Mateo is invited to free after-school tutoring. Read the Spanish. What does the tutoring line say?

Hola familia de Mateo, Estamos empezando una unidad sobre razones y tasas. Mateo debe asistir a tutoría después de la escuela los martes y jueves de 3:15 a 4:00. Habrá un examen el 25 de septiembre. Si tienes preguntas, mándame un mensaje. Sra. Petrova
Locked until you answer.
Hello Mateo's family, We are starting a unit on ratios and rates. Mateo must attend tutoring after school on Tuesdays and Thursdays from 3:15 to 4:00. There will be a test on September 25. If you have questions, send me a message. Mrs. Petrova

"Invited to free tutoring" became "must attend." A family reads this tonight and thinks their son is in trouble. Nothing in Alina's English draft said that. The AI translated the words and lost the meaning.

Translated is not the same as understood. Back-translation just showed you that. Hold onto that tool. You'll find out later where it stops working.

Step 2 of 10 Prompt primer 2 min

Before you look at Alina's prompt

A prompt is the only instruction the tool gets. Anything you don't say, it decides for you.

A prompt controls

What to make, for whom, what to keep, what tone to use, and what language to write in.

The tool decides the rest

If you say "simplify," it picks what to cut. If you say "translate to Creole," it picks which Creole, or none.

Two verbs that matter here. "Translate this letter" and "write this letter in Spanish for a family" produce different letters. The first swaps words. The second has to think about the reader.
Step 3 of 10 Deconstruct the prompt 4 min

Here's exactly what Alina typed.

Simplify this ratios and rates problem set for my two ELs. Also write a short letter home about the unit and after-school tutoring, then translate it to Spanish and to Creole. [problem set pasted below]

Tap everything that's missing from this prompt. Some of these are already there. Pick carefully.

The prompt names the task and mentions two ELs, tutoring, and both languages. It never says who the students are, what rigor to keep, how the letter should sound, or that "Creole" needs to mean Haitian Creole, written for a Haitian family. Every failure you're about to find traces back to one of those gaps.

Alina's original set (what every other student got)
  • 12 problems: 8 one-step, 4 two-step
  • Uses the words ratio, rate, per, equivalent
  • One problem uses a ratio table
  • One worked example at the top
  • Sample one-step: "A car goes 240 miles in 4 hours. What is the rate in miles per hour?"
  • Sample two-step: "A recipe uses 3 cups of flour for every 2 cups of sugar. How much flour for 10 cups of sugar, and how many cups total?"
What the AI produced (Mateo's copy and Widlene's copy are identical)
  • 8 problems: 8 one-step, 0 two-step
  • "rate" became "how fast," "per" became "for each," "equivalent" became "the same as"
  • The ratio table problem is still there, same numbers
  • The worked example is still there
  • Sample: "A car goes 240 miles in 4 hours. How fast is it going each hour?"
  • The recipe problem, and the other three two-step problems, do not appear

How to fill the board. Each row is one feature of the original. Read the two middle columns, then click "Your call" and choose: Kept means it's there and unchanged. Changed means it's there but different. Gone means it doesn't appear at all. Fill every row, then score it.

40%of the original's rigor made it into the scaffolds (2 of 5 features kept as-is)

Read the board top to bottom.

Row 1, two-step problems: gone. The original had four. The scaffold has zero. Two-step problems are where 6th-grade ratio reasoning actually lives; one-step problems are 4th and 5th grade skills.
Row 3, academic vocabulary: changed. "Rate" became "how fast." The word the standard asks students to learn is the word the tool removed.
Row 5, problem count: changed. 12 became 8, and the 4 that disappeared were all the hard ones.

Rows 2 and 4 are fine. The ratio table and the worked example survived. So the tool didn't wreck the set. It quietly lowered it by two grade levels while keeping enough of the shape that it looks like a scaffold. "Simplify" did exactly what it says: it made the task simpler, not more accessible.

One more question, and it's the bigger one.

Look at the two middle columns again. Mateo's scaffold and Widlene's scaffold are word-for-word identical. Now look at the two profile cards at the top of this screen. Mateo does math well in Spanish and is at the start of learning English. Widlene speaks English all day and gets stuck on words like "equivalent." Those are opposite problems. Before you answer, ask yourself: what does each of these students need that the other one doesn't?

Which student is this scaffold actually built for?

For Mateo, the scaffold removed the math and kept the English. He needed the opposite: the same 12 problems, with the instructions in Spanish and a glossary so he can bring the math he already has.
For Widlene, the scaffold removed the very words she's supposed to learn. She needed those words taught: defined in plain English and practiced in sentence starters, with the content untouched.

"Simplify for my two ELs" told the tool there was one kind of student. There were two. That's the sentence you'll fix on the next screen.

Monday, 7:40 a.m. Message from Samuel Achebe

Widlene's aunt called the front office this morning. She wanted to know why the school sent them a letter in French. The family speaks Haitian Creole. She could work out most of it, but her words were: "They don't know who we are."

It isn't Creole. It's French.

Back-translation passed because French translates to English perfectly. The glossary passed because there are no math terms. The only check that could catch this was a person who knows the family and the language. Many Haitian families read some French, so this letter isn't gibberish. It's worse: it's a letter that tells the family the school didn't know who they are.

Remember this moment.

The check that worked on the Spanish letter did nothing here. Back-translation is a smoke detector, not a proof. When you can't read the language, the interpreter is the interpreter.

One finding

While you did the missions, the tool got at least one thing wrong. Write down the one you caught and how you caught it. Think about: a problem it removed or simplified without saying so, a word it translated with the everyday meaning instead of the math meaning, a tone that shifted (an invitation that became a requirement), a claim it made in Mission 2 that didn't match what it actually did, or something it admitted in Mission 4 that it couldn't verify.
