Step 1 of 10. Tomorrow's lesson. About 4 min

Lesson 3.4: What happens when a population disappears?

Objective. Students construct an explanation, using evidence from a food-web model, predicting how removing one population affects at least two others, and reasoning about the energy pathways that connect them.

Cognitive demand. Construct an explanation from model evidence. Not recall. Not labeling.

  1. Warm-up, 5 min: pairs sort nine organism cards into producer, consumer, decomposer.
  2. Diagram, 15 min: temperate forest food web (oak, grass, grasshopper, rabbit, mouse, snake, hawk, fox, fungi); trace three energy pathways.
  3. Passage, 10 min: 250 words on a documented pattern: a mid-level predator declines, its prey rises, that prey's food source declines.
  4. CER task, 15 min: "The snake population is removed. Predict what happens to two other populations. Support it with evidence from the diagram and the passage." One written paragraph.
  5. Exit, 5 min: which pathway was most affected, and why.

Grade 7 life science. This is competent, ordinary teacher work, and it is fine for most of the room. You are adapting it, not fixing it. Read it now; in a minute you will read it again through five students' eyes.

Step 8 of 10. What was planted. About 10 min, plus 5 if you revise

Two real failures. One decoy.

Failure 1

Lucía: language mistaken for thinking

The assistant made a version for a beginner. It could not ask "beginner at what?" Lucía is a beginner at English and an intermediate at ecosystems, and the tier collapsed the second into the first. Labeling and matching are not a scaffolded explanation. They are the removal of the explanation.

Anywhere: any proficiency label (language level, Lexile, grade band, "struggling") gets treated as a ceiling on thinking instead of a constraint on access. When a draft simplifies, ask which demand it simplified.

Failure 2

Marcus: the label, not the IEP

Two of four items were good, which is what made the other two dangerous. The shortened, simplified passage replaces grade-level text his IEP gives him access to through text-to-speech: a modification, not an accommodation, and not yours to make. The single-word organizer ignores his oral-response accommodation and caps his output below the task.

Anywhere: the model turns a binding document (IEP, 504, policy, grant terms) into a vibe and generates from the vibe. Check the draft against the document's actual terms.

The decoy

Lucía writing her reasoning in Spanish

Defensible. The construct is scientific explanation, not English composition; the English claim frame keeps academic English present. If you flagged it, you applied the rule "reject anything easier." That is the same instinct that produced Failure 1, pointed the other way. The rule is "find out what changed."

Also in the draft, not planted

Priya's pond web was volume, not depth. Depth is an indirect effect (what happens to the oak?) or defending a prediction against a rival. Jayden's five-minute pre-teach was sound: it addresses the missing prerequisite instead of shortening the task.

A Proficient tiered lesson set, for comparison

Constant: objective as written; every student explains the effect of removing the snake on two populations from diagram and passage evidence.

Marcus: original passage with text-to-speech, chunked with headers, snake pathways highlighted; CER paragraph by voice or speech-to-text. Source: IEP.

Lucía: diagram with Spanish-English glossary; key sentence in both languages; claim in English, reasoning in Spanish or as an annotated diagram. Source: proficiency level, transcript.

Priya: same web; trace one indirect effect two links away and defend it against a counter-prediction. Source: pre-assessment.

Jayden: five-minute pre-teach with cards plus a worked CER example, then the standard task with the class frame. Source: attendance.

Amara: standard task with a required "because" sentence linking evidence to claim. Source: last two CERs.

Rejected: simplified passage (modification); single-word boxes (ignores accommodation 2); labeling tier (removes the explanation); pond web (volume).

Facilitator note

The misconception most teachers bring in: that differentiation means easier work for some and more work for others. It shows in the first-pass triage: the Lucía labeling tier gets "Keep" because it looks kind, the Priya pond web gets "Keep" because it looks rigorous. The Lab makes that instinct fail twice, once in each direction.

Two questions that start good discussion. "What did the assistant know about Lucía when it wrote her tier, and what didn't it know?" And: "Who is allowed to decide that Marcus reads a different passage?" The honest answer, the IEP team and not the classroom teacher alone, is the point of the consultation field.

What a struggling participant looks like. Writes "the topic" for the constant. Keeps four or five first-pass drafts. Prompts by label again in the build and iterates zero times on Marcus. Flags the Spanish reasoning and nothing else in the audit. Spends the revision window on wording. The header score will show it: misses and over-corrections both up. That is a coaching conversation, not a grade.

The debrief point that must not be skipped: the decoy. If the debrief ends at "the AI made two mistakes," participants leave with the rule "flag anything easier," which is worse than the misconception they arrived with. Spend the time on why Lucía writing in Spanish is defensible.

The transfer step. Do not let anyone skip it. A teacher who catches the failure in our sandbox and never checks whether their own tool does the same thing is certified on our sandbox, not on their job. The pasted draft and pushback are the evidence; read them.

The sandbox is not instructed to make mistakes. The failures come from ordinary model behavior when prompted with labels. A teacher who pastes full profiles may get sound tiers first try; the audit guarantees exposure either way.
